Repairing an over-done alarplasty

An alarplasty cuts skin from the nostrils to make them smaller. That skin doesn't grow back. This guide explains, in plain words, the main repair options, what each one can and can't do, and what the research says.

Based on published studies, surgeons' public case photos and experiences patients have shared. Not medical advice.

Not sure where to start? Take the 3-question quiz →

Why this guide exists

Most of a rhinoplasty can be revised. Cartilage and bone can usually be rebuilt with grafts. An alarplasty is different: it cuts away skin, and skin doesn't grow back. That makes it one of the few steps in a nose job that can't truly be undone.

Yet many patients report waking up with an alarplasty they never agreed to. Surgery without consent is a serious violation of bodily autonomy, and patients describe lasting psychological harm, including not recognizing their own face.

This guide was put together by patients who went through it. They dug through the studies, compared hundreds of before and after photos and questioned surgeons, so the next person in the same situation doesn't have to start from zero.

Before you start

What did the alarplasty take?

The right repair depends on what's missing. Two different cuts cause two different problems.

What's actually wrong?

"My nostrils look smaller" can mean four different problems. Most people have more than one, and each points to a different repair.

The shape is wrong

A notch, a teardrop or a flat, pinched wing. You may still have enough skin, but it sits in the wrong shape.

2a. Alar lobule flap

The support is gone

The wing caves in, or the rim is pulled up, because cartilage was trimmed or a scar is pulling. Cartilage can bring back the curve without adding skin.

3. Support and lining fixes

Skin is actually missing

The opening is smaller and the flare is gone because skin was cut away. This is the hardest problem: something has to add skin or move it in.

1. Composite grafts · 2b. Island flaps

The tip angle makes it look worse

A tip turned up shows more of the inside of the nostrils, so notches stand out. Changing the angle can hide them, but it can't put skin back.

4. Derotation

Two ways to bring in skin

The trade-off with flaps: you need spare skin nearby, you make a new cut where it comes from, and small flaps can shrink unless cartilage holds them in shape.

Find out what was actually done

Operative reports sometimes leave out or understate the alarplasty. In one case, revision surgeons measured about 5 mm taken from each side, where the original surgeon claimed 2 mm. Get an independent surgeon to examine you and write down the measurements first.

1

Composite grafts

Most studiedAdds real skinSkin + cartilage from the ear

Take a small piece of your ear that has two layers, skin and the cartilage under it, and stitch it into the nose to replace what was cut.

Composite graft vs. cartilage graft

"Ear graft" can mean two different things. A cartilage graft is cartilage only: it can prop up or reshape a wing, but it adds no skin, so it can't undo an alarplasty on its own. A composite graft is skin plus cartilage (sometimes skin and fat). It's the only kind of graft that puts skin back. This section is about composite grafts.

A graft is like a cut flower in a vase. It has no roots, so it has to grow new blood vessels in its new spot. Small ones usually make it. Big ones sometimes don't.

47 of 47small grafts took. 56 patients, many after too much alar base was cut, followed for 4 years 8 months on average (Manafi 2013).
93.7%of 367 grafts in rhinoplasty patients took fully (Toriumi 2023).

Most composite graft studies are about injuries, burns and cancer, not cosmetic alarplasty. Manafi's is one of the few that includes over-cut alar bases.

Surviving isn't the whole story

Most grafts take, especially small ones. But for a cosmetic repair the graft also has to look like your own skin. Shrinking, color, texture and the scar line matter even when the graft is alive.

1a

Internal composite grafts

The middle ground

The wing is released from the inside, scar tissue is freed, and the composite graft is slipped into the nostril lining so the wing can sit further out. It doesn't rebuild the outside of the wedge, but it adds some size and flare without an outside scar. It sits between doing nothing and an outside patch.

Surgeons who have posted cases: Dr. Oguzhan Oguz (Turkey), Dr. Shahriar Nazari (Iran) and Dr. Ahmet Halit Aydın (Turkey).

1b

External composite grafts

Works, but shows

The composite graft is sewn into the outside of the wing, right where the wedge or sill was cut. It's the most direct way to rebuild the original shape, and it has decades of studies behind it. The catch is the outside scar, and ear skin doesn't always match nose skin, especially on darker skin.

Surgeons who have posted or published cases: Dr. Jason Roostaeian (US), Dr. Amir Sazgar (Iran), Dr. Kidakorn Kiranantawat (Thailand) and Dr. Ali Manafi (Iran, in his published study).

Skin tone matters

Ear skin can heal lighter or darker than the skin around it, and scars hold less pigment. On darker skin that's easier to see. The studies are mixed: in one, pigment varied like a skin graft (Ahuja 2014); in another, over 90% of grafts survived in skin types III to V (Agrawal 2025). There's almost no data for the darkest skin types. If you have darker skin, ask to see healed results on people with your skin tone.

Bottom line. The most studied way to put skin back. If you want more size and can live with modest gains, an internal graft is the middle ground. External grafts rebuild the most, but the patch can show. Either way, grafts can shrink back over time.

What the research says

Composite grafts have more studies than anything else here, though most are about injuries, burns and cancer rather than cosmetic alarplasty.

Manafi et al., 2013 (Iran)
  • 56 patients, mostly after too much alar base was cut
  • Small grafts (skin and fat): 47 of 47 took
  • Bigger grafts with cartilage: 5 of 9 excellent, 3 acceptable, 1 failed
  • Average follow-up 4 years 8 months
  • Not advised over 2 cm. Use flaps instead
Toriumi et al., 2023 (US)
  • 367 grafts in 234 rhinoplasties
  • 93.7% took fully, 6.3% partly or fully failed
Sazgar et al., 2025 (Iran)
  • 45 people with over-cut nostril floor and wing junctions
  • No graft failures, even after past skin death
  • Breathing and appearance scores improved a lot
Ahuja et al., 2014 (India)
  • 19 patients, all survived, all shrank a little
  • Pigment varied, like a skin graft
  • Keep grafts under 1 cm wide
Agrawal et al., 2025 (India)
  • 11 patients, skin types III to V
  • Over 90% survival, one shrank by about half

2

Flaps

Skin that keeps its own blood supply

Move nearby skin into the nostril without ever cutting it off from its blood supply. Two very different sizes of flap:

2a

Alar lobule flap + ear cartilage

Best direct evidenceFixes shape, not size

If there's a little spare skin on the outside of the nostril wing, the surgeon slides it into the notch while it stays attached, and puts a curved piece of ear cartilage underneath to hold a rounder shape.

Like rearranging furniture instead of building a new room. Nothing new comes in, so the nostril doesn't get bigger, but everything sits in a nicer shape.

How it's done

The results

16patients, all treated for damage after a cosmetic nostril reduction
Every flapsurvived. One cartilage graft got infected and was removed.
2 to 3.5 mmof spare wing skin was the most a flap used

Ohba et al., 2024. The only study we found on exactly this problem: damage after cosmetic nostril reduction.

Can help if

  • You have notches, teardrop shapes or flat wings
  • There's a little spare skin on the outer wing
  • You want a natural curve more than a bigger opening

Won't help if

  • A lot of sill skin was cut
  • You mainly want bigger nostrils
  • There's no spare wing skin to move

Trade-offs

  • A small new scar, kept in the crease
  • Ear cartilage means a second small cut at the ear
  • A nostril retainer, worn for about 6 months

Not the same as making the nostril bigger

This flap moves skin you already have. It fills notches and brings back a natural curve. It doesn't replace a lot of skin that was cut away. In the study, any widening came from releasing a ligament and using cartilage to push the wings out, not from the flap itself.

Where: Dr. Norihiro Ohba, Primo Azabujuban Clinic, Tokyo. Dr. Göde and Dr. Tolga Bilece have also offered it to patients.

2b

Lip or cheek island flap

Promising, unprovenThe only option that adds living skin

A small island of skin from the upper lip or cheek is moved into the nostril while it stays attached to a stem of blood vessels, so the wing can be pushed back out.

Like moving a garden hose to another plant without ever unplugging it from the tap.

Dr. Sercan Göde (Izmir) has posted the most cases.

0published studies of this flap for repairing a cosmetic alarplasty
5patients: a flap from the crease moved a pulled-in nostril base outward (Patel 2022)

Why it's promising

  • The only option that brings new living skin, so in theory the most size
  • Can't die like a graft
  • Nearby skin usually matches better than ear skin

Reasons for caution

  • Very new for alarplasty. Most photos are from the operating table
  • Skin-only flaps can shrink and flatten the sills
  • Some posted results show clear scars on the sill or lip

Established surgery, new use

Moving skin on its own blood supply is standard in cancer reconstruction. Using it to reverse a cosmetic alarplasty isn't, yet: no long-term series shows how the scars, the shrinking and the extra width hold up.

What doctors have published

No study has tested lip island flaps for cosmetic alarplasty reversal yet, but the building blocks exist:

  • Tregaskiss et al., 2014: 103 patients had a wing rebuilt with an island flap from the side of the nose. No flap was lost, and most results were rated good to excellent.
  • Patel et al., 2022: a flap from the crease moved a pulled-in nostril base outward in 5 patients, with a rim graft for support.
  • Nadjmi et al., 2022: rebuilt a damaged wing with a flap from the opposite nostril base, with matching color.
  • Selçuk et al., 2012: cheek flaps with cartilage support for wing defects. Manafi points to flaps like these for defects over 2 cm.
  • Le Diep Linh et al., 2024: upper lip island flaps to rebuild columellas.

3

Support and lining fixes

Fixes support, not skinOften done first, or alongside

Rebuild the frame under the skin: cartilage to hold the wing's curve, and extra lining inside the nostril when the rim has been pulled up.

Like fixing a sagging tent by replacing the poles, not the fabric.

3.4 to 1.5 mmaverage rim pull-up before and after rim grafts plus V-Y advancement, in 25 patients (Zhan and Wang 2026)
2.7 mmaverage improvement in pulled-up rims with cartilage extension grafts, in 47 patients. Scarring limited 7 of them (Marianetti 2020).
Noneof these add outside skin. They fix collapse and pulled-up rims.
  • Cartilage grafts. Pulled-up or collapsed wings can often be fixed with cartilage before touching skin.
  • V-Y advancement. A small V-shaped flap of lining inside the nostril is slid forward and stitched as a Y, adding lining so the rim can come down. Paired with rim grafts, it worked well for pulled-up rims in a 2026 study. It adds inside lining, not outside skin.
  • Scar release. Loosening tight scars, sometimes with a Z-shaped cut, can help with smiling, tightness and itching. It won't make nostrils wider.
  • Composite rim grafts. For a notched or pulled-up rim, a small graft of ear skin and cartilage can be set into the rim. In a 2026 study of 15 patients with rim defects, no graft died and 87% were satisfied.
  • Scar care. Laser, dermabrasion, tretinoin and medical tattooing are all options, and results vary. Dermabrasion carries pigment risk on darker skin.
  • Wait for swelling to settle. Early on, nostrils can look smaller and stiffer than they'll end up.

Bottom line. Often the first step, and often done alongside the other repairs. It fixes collapse and pulled-up rims. It doesn't replace skin that was cut away.

4

Derotation

Helper onlyOnly if your tip was turned upAdds no tissue

Tip the point of the nose down a little, so less of the inside of the nostrils shows.

Think of a mug. Tilt it up and you see right inside. Tilt it down and you don't. Derotation tilts the nose down so the uneven nostrils hide.

Why it's last: no study has tested derotation as an alarplasty repair, and it can't put back skin that was cut. It can make what you have look its best, usually alongside one of the repairs above.

Can help if

  • Your tip got turned up in surgery
  • Your alarplasty was mild to moderate
  • Your nostrils look better when you push the tip down

Won't help if

  • A lot of skin was cut
  • Your tip wasn't turned up
  • Your wing was stitched too low compared to the columella

Trade-offs

  • Usually needs a strong support graft (an SEG, often rib), which can feel stiff
  • Overdone, it can look older or droopy
  • It's another full nose surgery

More detail Why it might work, patient experiences, sources

Why it might help

Alarplasty damage is easiest to see when a lot of the nostril shows. Many patients also have their tips turned up in the same surgery, which puts the notches and uneven sides on display. Surgeons do treat nostril show as a real, fixable problem and even classify how the rims sit (Rohrich and Afrooz, 2019). But nobody has published derotation as an alarplasty repair. The idea comes from patients comparing hundreds of before and after photos.

The mirror test

In a mirror, gently push your nose tip down using your upper lip and cheek muscles. If the wings flare out more, rotation is probably part of your problem. If nothing changes, you're most likely missing skin. It's a rough clue, not a diagnosis.

What patients report

  • If there is more rotation, there is more nostril show. So you see the alarplasty deformity more easily.A patient who researched derotation
  • With severe alar issues, simple derotation will not fix the issue.A patient with severe alarplasty damage, about their own case. Derotation pulled their nostrils further up, not out.

Before you book

Questions to ask any surgeon

These move the talk away from vague words like "reversal" and towards exactly what will be done.

  1. What exactly is missing?

    Outside skin, inside lining, cartilage support? Or is the tissue there but scarred into the wrong shape? Is my tip turned up? Each has a different fix.

  2. Draw every cut on my photo

    On a photo of my nose from below: where does new tissue come from, where does it go, and where will every scar be? Inside or outside?

  3. What keeps it alive?

    For a graft: how big is it, and what will feed it? For a flap: where is its blood supply?

  4. What keeps it from shrinking back?

    Cartilage support, scar release, a retainer? What have you seen after 12 months? Grafts and flaps can shrink over time.

  5. Show me the same operation, healed

    From below and from the front, 6 to 12 months or more later, on skin like mine and a problem like mine. Not on the operating table. Can I talk to them?

  6. What if we do less?

    A smaller fix for the shape may be safer than chasing every lost millimeter.

  7. How many millimeters, exactly?

    How much will you add or remove, and will it be written in my report?

  8. Stitches and scar care

    Which stitches will you use outside, and when do they come out? Facial stitches usually come out around day 7. What's the plan for scars afterwards?

  9. Can it be undone, and is it all in writing?

    If I don't like it, what then? Will you do anything I haven't agreed to in writing? Some patients have woken up with an alarplasty they never asked for.
