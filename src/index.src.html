Repairing an over-done alarplasty

An alarplasty cuts skin from the nostrils to make them smaller. That skin doesn't grow back. This guide explains, in plain words, the repair options our patient group found, what each one can and can't do, and what the research says.

Made by patients, for patients. Based on 8,078 messages in our research group chat (March to October 2026) and published studies. Member names removed. Not medical advice.

Before you start

What did the alarplasty take?

The right repair depends on what's missing. Two different cuts cause two different problems.

Each method below helps with some of these and not others:

  • Shape: a round nostril instead of a notch, a teardrop or a flat, pinched wing.
  • Size: a bigger opening. This needs new tissue, because the old tissue is gone.
  • Flare: wings that curve outward. The wedge cut is what usually takes this.
More on this Notch types, reports, an example

Not every notch is the same notch

  1. Weak cartilage. The wing caves in because its support was trimmed. Usually fixable with cartilage grafts.
  2. A rim that's pulled up (retraction). Often fixable with grafts placed inside the nose.
  3. Skin cut away by alarplasty. The shortage is on the outside, and there's no easy fix. This is most of us.

Find out what was actually done

Many of us got reports that left out or understated the alarplasty. One member's revision surgeons measured about 5 mm taken from each side, where his original surgeon claimed 2 mm. Get an independent surgeon to examine you and write down the measurements first.

1

Derotation

A helper, not a fixOnly if your tip was turned upKeeps your current tissue

Tip the point of the nose down a little, so less of the inside of the nostrils shows.

Think of a mug. Tilt it up and you see right inside. Tilt it down and you don't. Derotation tilts the nose down so the uneven nostrils hide.

Can help if

  • Your tip got turned up in surgery
  • Your alarplasty was mild to moderate
  • Your nostrils look better when you push the tip down

Won't help if

  • A lot of skin was cut
  • Your tip wasn't turned up
  • Your wing was stitched too low compared to the columella

Trade-offs

  • Usually needs a strong support graft (an SEG, often rib), which can feel stiff
  • Overdone, it can look older or droopy
  • It's another full nose surgery

Bottom line. Only worth it if your tip was turned up. It makes what you have left look its best, often in the same surgery as other repairs. It won't bring back what was cut.

More detail Why it might work, member experiences, sources

Why it might help

Alarplasty damage is easiest to see when a lot of the nostril shows. Many of us also had our tips turned up in the same surgery, which puts the notches and uneven sides on display. Surgeons do treat nostril show as a real, fixable problem and even classify how the rims sit (Rohrich and Afrooz, 2019). But nobody has published derotation as an alarplasty repair. The idea came from us comparing hundreds of photos.

The mirror test

In a mirror, gently push your nose tip down using your upper lip and cheek muscles. If the wings flare out more, rotation is probably part of your problem. If nothing changes, you're most likely missing skin. It's a rough clue, not a diagnosis.

What members found

  • If there is more rotation, there is more nostril show. So you see the alarplasty deformity more easily.The member who started the derotation idea
  • Nostril flare can be currently improved with downrotation. Flaps and skin grafts do not help for flare.A member with eight surgeries, who was against derotation at first
  • With severe alar issues, simple derotation will not fix the issue.The same member, months earlier, about her own case. Derotation pulled her nostrils further up, not out.

One member's nostrils looked wider after his revision even though their size didn't change: his surgeon made the tip stick out less and propped up the collapsed wings. Later, a surgeon told the same member his real issue was how the rim had been stitched, not rotation, and a US surgeon turned him down, saying his skin might not allow the derotation he wanted. Get examined before betting on this.

US surgeons named most often for strong derotation: Dr. Sam Most and Dr. Ari Hyman. Dr. Scott Owen and Dr. Steitz also came up.

2

Composite grafts

Skin + cartilage from the earThe most studied option

Take a small piece of your ear that has two layers, skin and the cartilage under it, and stitch it into the nose to replace what was cut.

Composite graft vs. cartilage graft

"Ear graft" can mean two different things. A cartilage graft is cartilage only: it can prop up or reshape a wing, but it adds no skin, so it can't undo an alarplasty on its own. A composite graft is skin plus cartilage (sometimes skin and fat). It's the only kind of graft that puts skin back. This section is about composite grafts.

A graft is like a cut flower in a vase. It has no roots, so it has to grow new blood vessels in its new spot. Small ones usually make it. Big ones sometimes don't.

2a

Internal composite grafts

The middle ground

The wing is released from the inside, scar tissue is freed, and the composite graft is slipped into the nostril lining so the wing can sit further out. It doesn't rebuild the outside of the wedge, but it adds some size and flare without an outside scar. This is where most of our group landed.

Surgeons reported as doing it internally: Dr. Oguzhan Oguz, Dr. Shahriar Nazari (Iran), Dr. Blas García (Spain), Dr. Kidakorn Kiranantawat (Thailand), Dr. Ahmet Halit Aydın, and reportedly Dr. Khosh (US). Dr. Göde says he now only does them internally.

2b

External composite grafts

Works, but shows

The composite graft is sewn into the outside of the wing, right where the wedge or sill was cut. It's the most direct way to rebuild the original shape, and it has decades of studies behind it. The catch is the outside scar, and ear skin doesn't always match nose skin, especially on darker skin.

Surgeons reported as doing it externally: Dr. Jason Roostaeian (US), Dr. Amir Sazgar (Iran), Dr. Shingyochi (Japan), Dr. Erkan Soylu, Dr. Fatullayev, and Dr. Ali Manafi (in his published study).

Skin tone matters

Ear skin can heal lighter or darker than the skin around it, and scars hold less pigment. On darker skin that's easier to see. Members saw external composites heal badly on Black and East Asian patients, and Dr. Toriumi told a Black member: "if you were Caucasian, I would consider, but as a black person, I wouldn't recommend it." The studies are mixed, and there's almost no data for the darkest skin types. If you have darker skin, ask to see healed results on people with your skin tone, and lean towards internal.

Bottom line. If you want more size and can live with modest gains, an internal composite graft is the middle ground. External grafts rebuild the most, but the patch can show. Either way, grafts can shrink back over time.

More detail What surgeons said, research, figures, sources

What surgeons told members

  • Dr. Oguzhan Oguz (Istanbul, August 2026) told a member he'd only do internal grafts, never external, and advised against external flaps too. Another member said he suggested external for her, so ask directly.
  • Dr. Sercan Göde said he used to do many composite grafts but now prefers flaps, because grafts must grow a new blood supply and can shrink.
  • Dr. Erkan Soylu turned down one member because he only does it when the nostrils are very small. Someone in another patient group widened successfully with him.
  • Dr. Ziad Katrib uses internal grafts for pulled-up rims but turned down alarplasty repair.

What the research says

Composite grafts have the most evidence of anything here, though most studies are about injuries, burns and cancer rather than cosmetic alarplasty.

Manafi et al., 2013 (Iran)
  • 56 patients, mostly after too much alar base was cut
  • Small grafts (skin and fat): 47 of 47 took
  • Bigger grafts with cartilage: 5 of 9 excellent, 3 acceptable, 1 failed
  • Not advised over 2 cm. Use flaps instead
Toriumi et al., 2023 (US)
  • 367 grafts in 234 rhinoplasties
  • 93.7% took fully, 6.3% partly or fully failed
Sazgar et al., 2025 (Iran)
  • 45 people with over-cut nostril floor and wing junctions
  • No graft failures, even after past skin death
  • Breathing and appearance scores improved a lot
Asian patients, 2026
  • 15 patients, grafts placed from inside the nostril
  • Mild shrinking and color mismatch at 3 to 6 months
  • Scars "almost indistinguishable" at 1 year; 87% satisfied
Ahuja et al., 2014 (India)
  • 19 patients, all survived, all shrank a little
  • Pigment varied, like a skin graft
  • Keep grafts under 1 cm wide
Agrawal et al., 2025 (India)
  • 11 patients, skin types III to V
  • Over 90% survival, one shrank by about half

A fresh composite graft can look pale, then bluish around day 7, before it pinks up as blood vessels grow in (Jackson and Reisler, 2018). Ask your surgeon what to watch for.

3

Flaps

Skin that keeps its own blood supply

Move nearby skin into the nostril without ever cutting it off from its blood supply.

The trade-off: you need spare skin nearby, you make a new cut where it comes from, and small flaps can shrink unless cartilage holds them in shape.

3a

Alar lobule island flap

Gentlest optionFixes shape, not size

A tiny crescent of spare skin from the outside of the same nostril wing is moved into the notch, usually with a curved piece of ear cartilage to round out the wing. It uses tissue you already have and keeps the scar in the crease. You need a little spare skin on the outer wing for it to work.

Like rearranging furniture instead of building a new room. Nothing new comes in, so the nostril doesn't get bigger, but everything sits in a nicer shape.

Where: Dr. Norihiro Ohba, Primo Azabujuban Clinic, Tokyo. Dr. Göde and Dr. Tolga Bilece have also offered it to members.

More detail The study, more figures, what members learned
The study (Ohba et al., 2024)
  • 16 patients, 2016 to 2022, all after cosmetic nostril reduction
  • Main tool: curved ear cartilage, chosen to avoid the color problems of composite grafts
  • Flap added only when there was spare outer wing skin, 2 to 3.5 mm wide at most
Results
  • Every flap survived
  • Nostril shape improved in every case
  • 1 cartilage graft got infected and was removed
  • Typical follow-up about 9 months; retainer worn 6 months
Good to know
  • To widen, the authors release a ligament and use cartilage to push the wings outward
  • Small study, run by the clinic's own surgeons
  • Members stress it needs cartilage support or the groove can flatten

For tight scars and tethering next to the wings, members also liked the Japanese surgeon @tetuichiro, though he wasn't taking overseas patients when asked.

3b

Lip or cheek island flap

Promising, unprovenThe only option that adds living skin

A small island of new skin from the upper lip or cheek is moved into the nostril while still attached to a stem of blood vessels, so the wing can be pushed back out. Dr. Sercan Göde (Izmir) is the surgeon we've seen do this most. It works well for rebuilding columellas, and he started using it for alarplasty reversal in 2026.

Like moving a garden hose to another plant without ever unplugging it from the tap.

Why we're interested

  • The only option that brings new living skin, so in theory the most size
  • Can't die like a graft
  • Nearby skin usually matches better than ear skin

Why we're waiting

  • Very new for alarplasty. Most photos are from the operating table
  • A member who spoke to 20+ lip flap patients says their bases narrowed again within weeks
  • Skin-only flaps can shrink and flatten the sills
  • Some patients got clear scars on the sill or lip

More detail How it works, what surgeons said, research, 7 more examples

How it was explained to a member

The surgeon cuts along the crease, keeps that skin attached, and turns it in to make a new sill, which lets the wing move outward. Cartilage or an internal graft goes underneath for support. Dr. Göde told a member he'd turn a lip flap toward the wing rim to add width. He also said he hadn't done it on a Black patient yet.

What other surgeons said

  • Dr. Oguz: he's done cheek (nasolabial) flaps for cancer patients and the scars look bad; flaps are too much just for flare.
  • A US surgeon: does flap reconstruction often for cancer patients but would never recommend it for cosmetic patients; his note called flap rotations for alarplasty "very risky."

What doctors have published

No study has tested lip island flaps for cosmetic alarplasty reversal yet, but the building blocks exist:

  • Patel et al., 2022: a flap from the crease moved a pulled-in nostril base outward in 5 patients, with a rim graft for support.
  • Nadjmi et al., 2022: rebuilt a damaged wing with a flap from the opposite nostril base, with matching color.
  • Selçuk et al., 2012: cheek flaps with cartilage support for wing defects. Manafi points to flaps like these for defects over 2 cm.
  • Le Diep Linh et al., 2024: upper lip island flaps to rebuild columellas.

Bottom line. For notches with spare wing skin, the alar lobule flap with cartilage is the safest bet we found. Lip and cheek island flaps are the only real hope for lots of tissue, but there are almost no healed, long-term results yet. Several of us plan to try gentler fixes first and wait.

4

Other options

Usually alongside methods 1 to 3
  • Fix the structure first. Pulled-up or collapsed wings can often be fixed with cartilage grafts before touching skin. One study improved pulled-up rims by about 2.7 mm on average, though heavy scarring limited some results.
  • V-to-Y advancement. A small flap inside the nostril pushed outward, roughly 2 to 3 mm per side. Few surgeons offer it, and a member saw a botched case with outside scars.
  • Scar release. Loosening tight scars, sometimes with a Z-shaped cut, can help with smiling, tightness and itching. It won't make nostrils wider.
  • Scar care. Laser, dermabrasion, tretinoin and medical tattooing all helped some members and not others. Dermabrasion carries pigment risk on darker skin.
  • Wait for swelling to settle. Early on, nostrils can look smaller and stiffer than they'll end up.

Research to watch

Verteporfin, a drug being studied for healing with less scarring (Dr. Michael Longaker's lab, Stanford), and lab-grown tissue that survives until it connects to blood vessels (EU research project). Neither is available as a treatment yet.

Before you book

Questions to ask any surgeon

  1. Is my problem rotation, a pulled-up rim, weak cartilage, or missing skin? Each has a different fix.
  2. Can I see results a year or more after surgery, not on the operating table? Several members saw grafts and flaps shrink back within weeks.
  3. Have you done this on someone with my skin tone? Can I talk to them?
  4. Will the graft or flap go inside or outside? Where will every scar be?
  5. What will hold the new shape? Ask whether cartilage support is part of the plan.
  6. Which stitches will you use outside, and when do they come out? Members were told facial stitches usually come out around day 7.
  7. What's your plan for scar care afterwards?
  8. Exactly how many millimeters will you add or remove, and will it be in my report?
  9. If I don't like it, can it be undone?
  10. Will you do anything I haven't agreed to in writing? Many of us woke up with an alarplasty we never asked for.

Word list What the doctor words mean
Ala (alae)
The nostril wing.
Alar lobule
The soft, rounded part of the wing near the bottom.
Alar crease
The groove where the wing meets your cheek.
Sill
The little floor at the bottom of each nostril.
Columella
The strip of skin between the nostrils.
Alarplasty
Surgery that cuts skin from the nostrils to make them smaller. Also called alar base reduction.
Weir excision
An alarplasty that cuts both the sill and a wedge from the wing.
Flare
How much the wings curve outward.
Notching, teardrop deformity
A dent in the nostril edge, or a nostril pulled into a teardrop shape.
Retraction
When the nostril rim is pulled up too high.
Nostril show
How much of the nostril openings you can see.
Rotation, derotation
Turning the nose tip up, or back down.
SEG
Septal extension graft. A strong cartilage piece that sets where the tip points.
Cartilage graft
Cartilage only. Adds support and shape, not skin.
Composite graft
Skin plus cartilage (or fat), moved as one piece. Adds skin.
Flap
Tissue moved while staying attached to its blood supply.
Island flap
A flap attached only by a small stem of blood vessels, so it can move further.
Table photo
A photo taken on the operating table. Results look better than they'll end up.
Fitzpatrick type
A scale from I (very fair) to VI (deeply pigmented). Matters for scars and pigment.
